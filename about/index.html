About

A working notebook, opened up

This is a directory of 211 tools, grants, programmes and things worth reading for people starting a company, organised by the stage you're at rather than the software category the tool belongs to.

Why stages

A first-time founder does not know they need attribution software. They know they've launched and nobody is coming back. Category-first directories assume you already have the vocabulary for your problem, which is exactly what you don't have the first time. So the organising principle here is the question you're actually asking this week, and the answer is two or three resources rather than forty.

The twelve stages are a rough sequence, not a pipeline. Most people are in three of them at once and going backwards is normal — you validate again after you launch, you rebrand after you find the real customer.

Where it came from

It started as a running list of links accumulated over years of building things — the kind of note that grows to hundreds of lines, half of it saved at 2am and never opened again. Every entry was used or researched by someone building a real company. That's the entire credibility claim, and it's why the design looks like a notebook rather than a storefront.

Maintained in the open. The contributors are whoever has sent a pull request —the list is public, like everything else here.

No paid placement, ever

Nobody has paid to be in this directory, and nobody can. There is no sponsored slot, no boosted position, no "featured" tier, and no arrangement where a tool gets a better description because of who they are. Ordering is by how many entries a category has, or by the order things were added — never by money.

If an affiliate or referral link is ever added, it gets an affiliate link chip on its card and its own line in the repository README. Today there are none. You can verify that yourself: the data is one JSON file, and the git history shows every change ever made to it.

Honest about uncertainty

49 of the 211 entries carry an unverified chip. That means the link or the description was inferred from a name in the original notes and nobody has confirmed it since. The tool might be misnamed, dead, or something other than what we've assumed.

Those entries are shown, not hidden, because a guess labelled as a guess is more useful than a gap. What they never do is pretend. An entry stops being unverified when a human opens the link, confirms the description is true, and adds a lastChecked date in a pull request.

A weekly job also pings every link and opens an issue listing anything that has started returning an error. Dead links get removed or fixed, not quietly left to rot.

How it's built

Astro, statically generated, deployed from main. No database, no accounts, no server. The data lives in data/resources.json and CI validates it on every commit — schema conformance, no duplicate links, no slug collisions, and a hard fail on any description over twelve words.

There is deliberately no voting, no saved lists and no user accounts. All three need moderation, and the repository already is the moderation layer.
